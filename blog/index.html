2/10/26

Just another rant.

I still distinctly remember the time where people were told to never give out any personal information to anybody over the internet. It was maybe 10 years ago that we were told that we should never use any personal information whatsoever, even for social media because it all could be linked back to you. What happened to that? Why is it becoming so acceptable these days to require people to identify themselves?

Let's look at what they're thinking, that'll probably be a good place to start. On Ofcom's website, the very first things that they put up is about protecting children. There can be a relatively reasonable argument that younger children probably shouldn't have access to things that a child shouldn't be seeing, like pornography, but I think that Ofcom is either lying blatantly about what they want, or that they are simply ignorant. My suspicion is that it's the latter, because I don't think that they have bad intentions necessarily.

Firstly and most importantly, requiring people to use an ID in order to access services that would otherwise be easy to access in the name of safety is paradoxical. Children can be blocked from things, but you're then putting everybody else at risk in the name of protecting children. There's already been a patheticly high number of data breaches from companies whose sole purpose is to provide a method to verify a person's age using their government ID card. The highest profile example of this was the 5CA hack that leaked "70000 users that may have had government-ID photos exposed, which our vendor used to review agre-related appeals." (Discord Press). I don't like preaching about how unsafe the cloud is, but... it is. There are so many vectors that somebody could look at if they want to breach the personal information of a large group of people with companies like 5CA alone. There's no conceivable way that Ofcom is ignorant to this, as many people who petitioned against the Online Safety Act had pointed out. The name "Online Safety" is either an unintentional misnomer or purposefully vague, because it's specifically about children.

The Online Safety Act also explores attacking misogyny as well as general online abuse. I think that these were added to the Online Safety Act to avoid the name being a misnomer, but in execution, still maintains its position. Misogyny, online abuse, and calls to violence are already against the rules of common social media. I think it's relatively safe to say that alot of people aren't browsing /b/ for their daily dose of hate or whatever else happens there. No matter how much you try, squashing things like secret ultra hate discord servers or niche boards will never work, because hateful people are probably going to keep being hateful and will keep trying to find ways to express that hate. It's not like a complex battle or anything, you just enforce the rules that you already have. While I don't think that it's bad to include explicitly banning hate, it does nothing beyond give more room for Ofcom to posture itself as more virtuous than what it's actually trying to do.

So can it be about child safety? I'd still argue no, because the execution of such an idea puts more than just adults at risk of unwillingly participating in data breaches. Some companies these days have been doing something like a face scan to guess the age of somebody, most notably in Roblox. It's a serious issue with Roblox in particular because they decided to implement age-group chatting rules, where you can only chat with people around your age group. I won't go on as much of a tangent this time, but I think that Roblox is genuinely the worst company in regards to user safety. However, I think it's still safe to link this back to the Online Safety Act, because it has kicked off the push for alot of age verification in other countries as well, like Australia, and some states in the USA. It's been an issue in the USA so Ofcom isn't the only group to blame, but I think that the mass virtue signaling campaign probably convinced others that doing the same was righteous.

For so many years, privacy and anonymity had always been king on the internet, it was the norm. But now I guess people just don't know what parental controls are? I mean, I was raised with not being allowed to talk on a mic in Xbox 360 Black Ops I lobbies. That's normal, it should be normal, it should be seen as the norm to help your children learn about online safety. These days, all normal phones and computers have some way to implement parental controls. Local, offline parental controls ought to be the solution since it puts nobody at risk, but I haven't seen any push from Ofcom telling parents to use the parental controls within. It's just been stuff about child safety and how morally just it is to try to defend kids at all cost or something ridiculous like that. Rather than exhausting the options that already exist, Ofcom has decided to press the huge red button that you have to unlock with 3 keys, which is a decision that I simply cannot justify no matter how much steelmanning I try to do.

2/7/26

I am getting sick and tired of the amount of times that I see another youtube video upload about how some company is suddenly moving to a subscription for everything. Everybody has a right to be mad about subscriptions, but there's something very specific that I want to complain about here.

This idea that we as a society think that it's socially acceptable for corporations to use a subscription for some of the most basic things, like owning a laptop, is absolutely ridiculous to me. I think that if it received more coverage from the media, there would be a metric ton of outcry about it, but the media simply doesn't cover it. They don't want to cover it because of how slow-rolling it is. It's boring. Nobody wants to hear about how Hewlett-Packard is now messing around with the idea of a subscription TO USE A LAPTOP rather than buying one because it doesn't affect them. Nobody wants to hear about how horrible the financies of such a subscription are, because of how quickly the amount you pay outpaces the cost of the actual machine itself.

I realize that not everybody wants to use some laptop with a Coffee-Lake CPU from 2018 or older because of how easy it is to fix. I understand how alot of people just want to use a computer, and that's it. As much as I try to encourage people buying used, it keeps the prices relatively low for me when I want to do things like upgrade a T480, so I'm really not trying to sound like I'm some person that's not taking advantage of how little the used market is actually explored. But even if we look at the used vs. new argument, the end result is still the same; you now own a computer that you can use for something. Hewlett-Packard is stripping that right to own the laptop you pay for by allowing people to use a subscription for a laptop that has bad hinges, a terrible keyboard, and a fragile frame that would desintegrate if dropped. To be clear, Hewlett-Packard is not the only one that is doing this, but they're the ones that incited this rant.

Certainly more people think that this is bad, right? Surely, because there has been outcry about subscriptions before. But nobody is saying anything about laptops, because they're getting too expensive these days, which actually brings me to another tangent about how horrible laptops are these days. I cannot for the life of me justify spending so much money on any new laptop, let alone the ones that are trying to be both ultra thin and powerful. As much as I may not like the software, Macintosh does it perfectly. Their silicon is still unmatched for laptops to this day, but for people to chase the same thing with an x86 processor has to be the easiest philosophy for me to reject. Not every laptop has to be a huge, thick laptop, and I can easily get behind that, but the problem arises when you try to look at the market, you have to choose either gaming or ultra thin portability because your local Best Buy doesn't sell anything else.

I've had my experience with huge laptops. I still to this day have an Acer Predator Helios 300 from 2018 that comparitively weighs a ton, but it's to the point where ports are just being removed willy nilly because it doesn't fit the philosophy of having an ultra portable and powerful machine. Why is there no middleground these days? Why is it that if I want a decently built laptop from Best Buy I have to get one with a dGPU? I firmly understand that there are many laptops online that do not fit this stereotype, but I am not the typical consumer when it comes to computers. I understand that used ThinkPads and Framework Laptops are the magnum opus of laptops, but others do not. Why do they have to be sold junk? You could argue that it's the responsibility of the consumer to do research, and while I would agree, it's also the responsibility of the storefront to have alot of variance for competition. If somebody can't find the laptop they researched in a local store, why would they order one online? It doesn't really make much sense for them, especially because it's far easier to trust a store because you walk in with money and you walk out with a computer.

I don't think that there is any single noble company that could outdo the tragedy of the Macbook. It was too appealing and thin, and now the consumer is paying the price of mimicry. I'm sick of it. I'm sick and tired of seeing all the advertisements about how thin and light a computer is, because it truly doesn't matter. Unless there's some disability in play here, I truly cannot understand why something like a T480 wouldn't be seen as a good thin laptop. This race to make computers paper thin will only make portable computing worse, and I will not stand for it. If that means that I'll have to keep using old computers or pay the premium price for niche brands who endorse repairability, then so be it. I will pay the price.
